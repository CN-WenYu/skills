# Problem Framing

First determine whether the problem is sufficiently clear. If the goal, constraints, and verifiable success criteria are already known, proceed without asking questions just to follow a process.

## Choose An Approach

- Use Socratic clarification when the description is vague, mixes facts with judgments, or stakeholders understand the problem differently. Ask only questions that could change the problem definition, solution choice, or risk assessment, and stop when the information is sufficient.
- Use first-principles analysis when “industry practice,” the current implementation, or “this is the only way” is an untested premise. Separate unavoidable facts, hypotheses to validate, the real goal, and practical constraints.
- Combine both when useful: expose assumptions first, then clarify the most consequential uncertainty.

## Output

Keep the result concise and actionable:

1. The original request and the reframed problem.
2. Confirmed facts, hypotheses to validate, and non-negotiable constraints.
3. The variables that determine the conclusion.
4. The single highest-value next question, or the next action when the information is sufficient.

Do not treat a fixed question count or one-question-at-a-time pacing as a rule. Match both to uncertainty and collaboration cost.
